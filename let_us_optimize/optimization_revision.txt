## 📚 WHAT I UNDERSTAND — THE OPTIMIZATION COURSE

### The Core Thesis of the Book

The book makes one central argument that ties everything together:

> **Optimization is moving downhill efficiently.** Every method is the same template: `x_{k+1} = x_k - t_k B_k^{-1} ∇f(x_k)`. The only difference between methods is **what you choose for `B_k`** — which is the same as choosing the geometry (metric) in which you measure "steepest."

**The five key ideas:**

1. **You never optimize f directly** — you optimize a truncated Taylor model of it
2. **A learning rate exists because a linear model has no bottom** — Newton doesn't need one because it uses a quadratic model
3. **Speed is set by the condition number κ** — the ratio of sharpest to flattest curvature
4. **With constraints, optimality is "no feasible descent direction"** — multipliers are exchange rates
5. **In deep learning, mechanisms survive but guarantees don't** — always know which you're quoting

### The 9 Core Ideas

<details>
<summary><b>Idea 1: Optimality is local</b> — Try your own first</summary>

**Model Answer:** Derivatives see only an infinitesimal neighbourhood, so every condition built from them certifies something local — convexity is the only thing that upgrades local to global.

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>Idea 2: Models, not functions</b> — Try your own first</summary>

**Model Answer:** You never optimize `f` directly — you optimize a truncated Taylor model of it; order 1 gives a direction, order 2 gives a complete step.

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>Idea 3: The learning rate exists because a line has no bottom</b> — Try your own first</summary>

**Model Answer:** A linear model decreases without bound, so first-order methods must be told how far to go; a quadratic model has a minimum, which is why Newton has a natural unit step.

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>Idea 4: Curvature caps the step</b> — Try your own first</summary>

**Model Answer:** An L-smooth function is trapped under a parabola of curvature L, so `t < 2/L` is safe and `t = 1/L` is the bound-minimizing choice.

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>Idea 5: κ is the tax</b> — Try your own first</summary>

**Model Answer:** The stiffest direction caps the step; the flattest one sets your progress; the mismatch costs a factor `κ = λ_max/λ_min` in iteration count.

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>Idea 6: "Steepest" is a choice</b> — Try your own first</summary>

**Model Answer:** The gradient represents the derivative through an inner product — change the metric and you change the steepest direction, giving Newton, Adam, sign-SGD and FGSM all at once.

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>Idea 7: Noise buys cheapness and costs accuracy</b> — Try your own first</summary>

**Model Answer:** A minibatch gradient is unbiased with variance `σ²/B`; you trade a factor `N/B` in cost for a noise floor you must shrink with a schedule.

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>Idea 8: Constraints weaken the condition on ∇f</b> — Try your own first</summary>

**Model Answer:** At a constrained optimum, ∇f need not vanish; it must lie in the cone of outward constraint normals — that containment is KKT.

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>Idea 9: A multiplier is a price</b> — Try your own first</summary>

**Model Answer:** µ*_i is how much the optimal value improves per unit you relax constraint i — weight decay, trust-region radii, and softmax denominators are all the same object.

</details>

---

### Part A — The Mathematical Floor

<details>
<summary><b>Convex Set</b> — Try your own first</summary>

**Model Answer:** A set where the straight segment between any two points stays inside — no dents, no holes.

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>Convex Function (3 Characterizations)</b> — Try your own first</summary>

**Model Answer:** A function where (1) the chord lies above the graph, (2) the tangent lies below the graph, or (3) the Hessian is positive semidefinite everywhere.

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>Why Convexity Matters</b> — Try your own first</summary>

**Model Answer:** For a convex function, a stationary point (∇f = 0) is a global minimum — local information becomes global.

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>Strong Convexity</b> — Try your own first</summary>

**Model Answer:** `f - (m/2)||x||²` is convex — the function is at least as curved as a parabola with curvature m.

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>The Polyak–Łojasiewicz (PL) Condition</b> — Try your own first</summary>

**Model Answer:** `(1/2)||∇f(x)||² ≥ µ(f(x) - p*)` — wherever you're far from optimal, the gradient is big; weaker than strong convexity, permits non-convexity, still gives linear rates.

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>The Gradient</b> — Try your own first</summary>

**Model Answer:** The gradient is not "the slope" — it's a machine that produces the slope in any direction you feed it: `D_v f(x) = ∇f(x)ᵀv`.

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>Why ∇f is Perpendicular to Level Sets</b> — Try your own first</summary>

**Model Answer:** Walking along a level set, f is constant so the directional derivative is zero — hence ∇f has no component along the level set, so it's perpendicular to it.

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>The Hessian — Two Interpretations</b> — Try your own first</summary>

**Model Answer:** `Hv` is how the gradient changes when you step along v; `vᵀHv` projects that change back onto v, giving the curvature you'd feel walking that way.

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>Rayleigh Quotient</b> — Try your own first</summary>

**Model Answer:** `vᵀHv / vᵀv` — the curvature in any direction is a weighted average of eigenvalues, trapped between λ_min and λ_max.

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>The Condition Number κ</b> — Try your own first</summary>

**Model Answer:** `κ = λ_max / λ_min` — the ratio of sharpest to flattest curvature; everything clever either reduces it or becomes blind to it.

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>The 4 Landscape Types</b> — Try your own first</summary>

**Model Answer:** Bowl (all λ > 0, small κ), Valley (all λ > 0, huge κ), Saddle (mixed signs), Plateau (λ ≈ 0 over a region).

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>Subgradient</b> — Try your own first</summary>

**Model Answer:** A vector s satisfying `f(y) ≥ f(x) + sᵀ(y-x)` for all y — at a kink, there are many supporting slopes, and `0 ∈ ∂f(x)` is the optimality condition.

</details>

---

### Part B — First-Order Methods

<details>
<summary><b>The Descent Lemma</b> — Try your own first</summary>

**Model Answer:** An L-smooth function is trapped under a parabola: `f(x+d) ≤ f(x) + ∇f(x)ᵀd + (L/2)||d||²`; the safe step is `t < 2/L`.

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>Gradient Descent Rate (Convex)</b> — Try your own first</summary>

**Model Answer:** `f(x_k) - p* ≤ L||x_0 - x*||² / (2k)` — sublinear, `O(1/ε)` iterations.

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>Gradient Descent Rate (Strongly Convex)</b> — Try your own first</summary>

**Model Answer:** Error shrinks by `(1 - 1/κ)` per step — linear, `O(κ log(1/ε))` iterations.

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>Gradient Descent Rate (Non-Convex)</b> — Try your own first</summary>

**Model Answer:** `min_{k<K} ||∇f(x_k)||² ≤ 2L(f(x_0)-f_inf)/K` — `O(ε⁻²)` to make the gradient small somewhere.

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>Line Search — Armijo Condition</b> — Try your own first</summary>

**Model Answer:** `f(x+td) ≤ f(x) + c₁·t·∇f(x)ᵀd` — forbids steps that are too long.

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>Line Search — Wolfe (Curvature) Condition</b> — Try your own first</summary>

**Model Answer:** `∇f(x+td)ᵀd ≥ c₂·∇f(x)ᵀd` — forbids steps that are too short; also keeps BFGS positive definite.

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>Momentum — The Mechanism</b> — Try your own first</summary>

**Model Answer:** Unroll the buffer `w_k = Σ βʲ g_{k-j}` — steady gradients amplify by `1/(1-β)`, alternating gradients damp by `1/(1+β)`; ratio ≈ 19 at β=0.9.

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>Momentum — The Rate</b> — Try your own first</summary>

**Model Answer:** Momentum replaces κ by √κ — `O(√κ log(1/ε))` iterations, turning the exponent from linear to square root.

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>Every Optimizer is One Template</b> — Try your own first</summary>

**Model Answer:** `x_{k+1} = x_k - t_k B_k^{-1} ∇f(x_k)` — methods differ only in B_k, which is a choice of geometry/metric.

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>The Adaptive Family Chain</b> — Try your own first</summary>

**Model Answer:** AdaGrad's sum → RMSProp's average → Adam's momentum + bias correction → AMSGrad's repair → AdamW's decoupled decay; each fixes a named defect.

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>Adam — What It Actually Does</b> — Try your own first</summary>

**Model Answer:** `m_k` estimates E[g], `v_k` estimates E[g²]; the ratio `m̂/√v̂` is a signal-to-noise ratio — consistent gradients get full steps, noise-dominated ones get damped.

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>Adam — Bias Correction</b> — Try your own first</summary>

**Model Answer:** Since `m_0 = v_0 = 0`, the estimates are too small by `1 - β^k`; dividing by this factor makes them unbiased — critical in the first ~1000 steps.

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>Why Adam is NOT Second-Order</b> — Try your own first</summary>

**Model Answer:** It divides by the size of the gradient `√E[g²]`, not by curvature `f''`; dimensional analysis: `g/√g²` is dimensionless, so the step carries units of `t` alone.

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>AdamW — Why We Need It</b> — Try your own first</summary>

**Model Answer:** With Adam, an L2 penalty added to the loss passes through the per-coordinate rescaling — coordinates with large gradients get less decay; AdamW applies decay outside the rescaling.

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>SGD — Unbiasedness</b> — Try your own first</summary>

**Model Answer:** `E_B[ĝ_B(θ)] = ∇F(θ)` — a minibatch gradient is unbiased; its variance is `σ²/B`, so quadrupling the batch halves the noise.

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>Why Noise Squares the Cost</b> — Try your own first</summary>

**Model Answer:** Deterministic: `O(ε⁻²)`. Stochastic: `O(ε⁻⁴)` — the noise term forces a balancing act between progress and variance; this exponent is unimprovable.

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>Robbins–Monro Conditions</b> — Try your own first</summary>

**Model Answer:** `Σ t_k = ∞` (you must travel far enough) and `Σ t_k² < ∞` (accumulated noise must be finite) — the step must decay, but not too fast.

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>Learning Rate Schedules</b> — Try your own first</summary>

**Model Answer:** Cosine (average step = t/2), linear-to-zero (nearly identical), inverse-√k (no fixed budget needed), step decay (abrupt drops), constant (debugging only).

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>Warmup — Two Reasons</b> — Try your own first</summary>

**Model Answer:** (1) Adaptive methods: `v_k` is a one-sample estimate at step 1, so early steps are noise; (2) Any method: sharpness L is largest at initialization, so the safe step is smallest at the start.

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>Gradient Clipping</b> — Try your own first</summary>

**Model Answer:** Norm clipping rescales `g` to length `c` when `||g|| > c` — preserves direction, caps magnitude; under `(L₀,L₁)`-smoothness it's the provably right algorithm, not a hack.

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>Critical Batch Size B_crit</b> — Try your own first</summary>

**Model Answer:** The batch size below which noise is what limits you; doubling B buys steps almost for free; above it you buy throughput but stop buying progress.

</details>

---

### Part C — Second-Order Methods

<details>
<summary><b>Newton's Method — Derivation</b> — Try your own first</summary>

**Model Answer:** Minimize the order-2 Taylor model: `m_k(d) = f(x_k) + ∇f(x_k)ᵀd + (1/2)dᵀH_kd`; setting derivative to zero gives `d = -H_k⁻¹∇f(x_k)` — a complete step with no learning rate.

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>Newton — Affine Invariance</b> — Try your own first</summary>

**Model Answer:** Change variables `x = Ay` and Newton visits exactly the same points in transformed space — it is blind to the condition number, unlike any first-order method.

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>Newton — Quadratic Convergence</b> — Try your own first</summary>

**Model Answer:** `||x_{k+1} - x*|| ≤ C||x_k - x*||²` — the number of correct digits roughly doubles per iteration, but only locally and only with full steps.

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>Why Deep Learning Doesn't Use Newton</b> — Try your own first</summary>

**Model Answer:** (1) Can't store/factor Hessian (O(n²) memory, O(n³) per step); (2) Minibatch noise corrupts curvature; (3) Landscape is saddle-dominated; (4) We use structured approximations (K-FAC, Shampoo) or nothing.

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>BFGS — The Secant Equation</b> — Try your own first</summary>

**Model Answer:** `B_{k+1}s_k = y_k` where `s_k = x_{k+1}-x_k`, `y_k = ∇f(x_{k+1})-∇f(x_k)` — the curvature model must reproduce the gradient change you just observed.

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>Why Wolfe Conditions are Required for BFGS</b> — Try your own first</summary>

**Model Answer:** Wolfe (C) implies `y_kᵀs_k > 0`; this implies `H_{k+1} ≻ 0`; this implies `d_{k+1}` is a descent direction — the chain that makes BFGS converge.

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>L-BFGS</b> — Try your own first</summary>

**Model Answer:** Never forms the Hessian — stores only the last m pairs `(s_i, y_i)` (m ≈ 5-20) and computes the product `H_k∇f` by a two-loop recursion in `O(mn)` time.

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>Trust Regions</b> — Try your own first</summary>

**Model Answer:** Minimize the model within a radius `||d|| ≤ Δ_k`; `ν` (the Lagrange multiplier) interpolates Newton (`ν=0`) and gradient descent (`ν→∞`) as the leash tightens.

</details>

---

### Part D — Constrained Optimization

<details>
<summary><b>The One Sentence of Constrained Optimization</b> — Try your own first</summary>

**Model Answer:** A point is optimal exactly when there is no feasible direction that decreases the objective — ∇f need not vanish; it must be resisted by the constraints.

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>Tangent Cone vs Linearized Cone</b> — Try your own first</summary>

**Model Answer:** Tangent cone = directions that are genuinely available; linearized cone = what calculus sees from first derivatives; a constraint qualification says the two agree.

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>Lagrange Multipliers — The Derivation</b> — Try your own first</summary>

**Model Answer:** Optimality makes ∇f perpendicular to every feasible direction; by `null(A)⊥ = range(Aᵀ)`, ∇f lies in the span of constraint gradients — the multipliers are its coordinates there.

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>Why ∇f ∥ ∇h at a Constrained Optimum</b> — Try your own first</summary>

**Model Answer:** Both are perpendicular to the same surface (its level set) — with one constraint, "both in the same 1D normal space" is parallelism.

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>The 4 KKT Conditions</b> — Try your own first</summary>

**Model Answer:** (1) Stationarity: forces balance; (2) Primal feasibility: you're actually inside; (3) Dual feasibility: µ ≥ 0 (walls push, never pull); (4) Complementary slackness: only touched walls exert force.

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>Complementary Slackness</b> — Try your own first</summary>

**Model Answer:** `µ_i * g_i(x*) = 0` — if a constraint is inactive (`g_i < 0`), its multiplier is zero (`µ_i = 0`); if a multiplier is positive (`µ_i > 0`), the constraint must be active (`g_i = 0`).

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>Necessity vs Sufficiency in KKT</b> — Try your own first</summary>

**Model Answer:** A minimum satisfies KKT (necessity) needs a constraint qualification; a KKT point is a minimum (sufficiency) needs convexity — conflating them is the most common error.

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>Constraint Qualifications</b> — Try your own first</summary>

**Model Answer:** LICQ (gradients independent), MFCQ (strict entering direction), Slater (strictly feasible point for convex problems) — all guarantee KKT multipliers exist.

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>Duality — Weak vs Strong</b> — Try your own first</summary>

**Model Answer:** Weak duality (`d* ≤ p*`) is always true — the player who moves second has the advantage; strong duality (`d* = p*`) requires convexity + Slater, and means a supporting line touches the value function.

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>Shadow Price</b> — Try your own first</summary>

**Model Answer:** `µ*_i = -∂p*/∂u_i` — a multiplier is the exchange rate between the objective and a constraint; large = expensive constraint worth renegotiating, zero = not binding.

</details>

---

### Part 4 — The Machine Learning Payoff

<details>
<summary><b>SVM Sparsity is Complementary Slackness</b> — Try your own first</summary>

**Model Answer:** Points beyond the margin have `g_i < 0` (inactive constraint), hence `α_i = 0` by complementary slackness, so they drop out of `w = Σ α_i y_i x_i` — sparsity is derived, not designed.

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>Weight Decay — 3 Readings</b> — Try your own first</summary>

**Model Answer:** The same λ is (1) a Lagrange multiplier of `||w||² ≤ C`, (2) a shadow price of that norm budget, and (3) an inverse prior variance — one scalar, three readings.

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>Backpropagation is the Adjoint Method</b> — Try your own first</summary>

**Model Answer:** The backpropagated deltas are Lagrange multipliers of the layer constraints `z_k = f_k(z_{k-1}, θ_k)` — a backward pass solves the adjoint system, which is why it costs about one forward pass.

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>Softmax is Constrained Entropy Maximization</b> — Try your own first</summary>

**Model Answer:** Maximize `-Σ p_k log p_k` subject to `Σ p_k = 1` and `Σ p_k z_k = constant`; the normalizing denominator is the exponentiated multiplier of the sum-to-one constraint.

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>ReLU is Projection onto a Convex Cone</b> — Try your own first</summary>

**Model Answer:** `ReLU(x) = max(0,x)` is the Euclidean projection onto the nonnegative orthant — the same operator as in projected gradient descent.

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>Implicit Bias of Gradient Descent</b> — Try your own first</summary>

**Model Answer:** On linearly separable data, unregularized logistic regression with GD converges in direction to the hard-margin SVM solution — the algorithm supplies a margin the objective never asked for.

</details>

---

### Diagnostics

<details>
<summary><b>The 5 Loss Curve Signatures</b> — Try your own first</summary>

**Model Answer:** Healthy (straight on log axis), Crawling (too small LR), Plateau (dead units or conditioning), Spiking (clip the gradient), Diverging (LR too high).

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>Overfit Eight Examples Test</b> — Try your own first</summary>

**Model Answer:** The single most valuable test — if the model can't overfit eight examples to zero loss, you have a bug, not a hyperparameter problem; run this before any tuning.

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>Theorem vs Habit</b> — Try your own first</summary>

**Model Answer:** Proved theorems (safe step, unbiased SGD) should be quoted; theorems under assumptions (acceleration, PL) are analogies in deep learning; empirical habits (warmup, cosine) are well-motivated but not proved — know which category a claim belongs to.

</details>

| Method | Update (per coordinate) |

| AdaGrad | `v ← v + g²`, `x ← x - t·g/√(v+ε)` |
| RMSProp | `v ← β₂v + (1-β₂)g²`, `x ← x - t·g/√(v+ε)` |
| Adam | `m ← β₁m + (1-β₁)g`, `v ← β₂v + (1-β₂)g²`; `m̂ = m/(1-β₁^k)`, `v̂ = v/(1-β₂^k)`; `x ← x - t·m̂/(√v̂+ε)` |
| AdamW | Adam step, then `x ← x - t·λ·x` outside rescaling |

**Defaults:** β₁=0.9, β₂=0.999, ε=1e-8

</details>

## 5. COMMON MISCONCEPTIONS — OPTIMIZATION

**How to use:** Read each misconception first. Try to identify what's wrong. Then click to reveal the reality.

---

### Misconception 1: "∇f = 0 means we found a minimum."

<details>
<summary><b>Click for Reality Check</b></summary>

| Misconception | Reality |
|---------------|---------|
| ∇f = 0 means we found a local minimum. | It means **stationary**. It could be a min, max, saddle, or plateau. In high dimensions, it's almost always a saddle. |

**Why this matters in deep learning:** With billions of parameters, the chance of all eigenvalues being positive is essentially zero. "The gradient got small" is not "we found a solution."

**Honest guarantee:** Non-convex GD only promises `min_{k<K} ||∇f(x_k)||² ≤ O(1/K)` — somewhere on the path, the gradient got small.

</details>

---

### Misconception 2: "Adam is a second-order method."

<details>
<summary><b>Click for Reality Check</b></summary>

| Misconception | Reality |
|---------------|---------|
| Adam uses second moments, so it's second-order. | Adam divides by the **size** of the gradient `√E[g²]`, not by **curvature** `f''`. Dimensional analysis: `g/√g²` is dimensionless, so the step carries units of `t` alone — Newton divides by `f''`, giving units of `g/f''`. |

**What Adam actually is:** A diagonal preconditioner using gradient **moments**, not curvature. It's a first-order method with per-coordinate scaling.

**The honest refinement:** For NLL losses, `E[ggᵀ]` under model-sampled labels is the Fisher matrix, which relates to curvature — but that's an approximation, not what Adam computes.

</details>

---

### Misconception 3: "Adam is proved to converge."

<details>
<summary><b>Click for Reality Check</b></summary>

| Misconception | Reality |
|---------------|---------|
| Adam has convergence guarantees. | The original Adam proof was **wrong**. There are convex counterexamples where Adam fails to converge. AMSGrad repairs that specific mechanism under extra assumptions. Adam's defence is **empirical**. |

**The practical consequence:** Adam works well in practice, but the guarantee is empirical, not theoretical. This is a defensible engineering choice, but should be stated honestly.

</details>

---

### Misconception 4: "Weight decay and L2 penalty are the same thing."

<details>
<summary><b>Click for Reality Check</b></summary>

| Misconception | Reality |
|---------------|---------|
| Weight decay and L2 regularization are identical. | **True for SGD, false for Adam.** With Adam, an L2 penalty passes through the per-coordinate rescaling — coordinates with large gradients get less decay. AdamW applies decay outside the rescaling. |

**Why this matters:** If you use L2 with Adam, the regularization strength depends on the gradient history. Use AdamW for actual weight decay.

</details>

---

### Misconception 5: "Momentum provably accelerates deep learning."

<details>
<summary><b>Click for Reality Check</b></summary>

| Misconception | Reality |
|---------------|---------|
| The acceleration theorem applies to deep learning. | The theorem is **convex** and **L-smooth**. In deep learning the **mechanism** (damping oscillations) is real and observable, but the **bound** does not apply. |

**What to say:** "Momentum accelerates" is an **ANALOGY** in deep learning. The mechanism transfers; the proof does not.

</details>

---

### Misconception 6: "Bigger batches always train faster."

<details>
<summary><b>Click for Reality Check</b></summary>

| Misconception | Reality |
|---------------|---------|
| Larger batches mean faster training. | Only up to **B_crit**. Below B_crit, doubling batch roughly halves steps. Above B_crit, you buy throughput but stop buying progress — you're paying compute for nothing. |

**The arithmetic:**
- Below B_crit: B × 2 → steps ÷ 2 (compute same)
- Above B_crit: B × 2 → steps barely change, compute × 2

</details>

---

### Misconception 7: "Warmup is just a hyperparameter you can skip."

<details>
<summary><b>Click for Reality Check</b></summary>

| Misconception | Reality |
|---------------|---------|
| Warmup is a hack you can tune away. | **Two independent mechanisms:** (1) Adam's variance estimate is a one-sample estimate at step 1; (2) sharpness L is largest at initialization, so the safe step is smallest at the start. Both are real. |

**What to say:** Warmup is not superstition. It's well-motivated engineering, though not a theorem.

</details>

---

### Misconception 8: "Gradient clipping is a hack."

<details>
<summary><b>Click for Reality Check</b></summary>

| Misconception | Reality |
|---------------|---------|
| Clipping artificially modifies gradients. | Under `(L₀,L₁)`-smoothness (curvature grows with gradient norm), clipping is the **provably better algorithm** than fixed-step GD. The classical theory's assumption (global L) was the wrong model. |

**The lesson:** When a practice is ubiquitous and absent from theory, the theory's assumption is usually wrong — not the practitioners.

</details>

---

### Misconception 9: "KKT always works."

<details>
<summary><b>Click for Reality Check</b></summary>

| Misconception | Reality |
|---------------|---------|
| KKT is the universal optimality condition. | **Necessity** needs a **constraint qualification** (LICQ, MFCQ, Slater). Without it, multipliers may fail to exist even at a genuine minimum (the cusp example). **Sufficiency** needs **convexity** — without it, KKT points are candidates only and may be saddles. |

**The one-sentence correction:** A minimum satisfies KKT (with CQ); a KKT point is a minimum (with convexity). Conflating the two is the most common error in constrained optimization.

</details>

---

### Misconception 10: "The Hessian test uses ∇²f alone."

<details>
<summary><b>Click for Reality Check</b></summary>

| Misconception | Reality |
|---------------|---------|
| Second-order condition: ∇²f ≻ 0. | Under constraints, the test uses `∇²_{xx}L` (Hessian of the **Lagrangian**), and only on the **critical cone** (directions you're allowed to move). The constraint surface's curvature enters through the multipliers. |

**Why:** Walking along a curved constraint changes f even if f itself is flat in that direction — the multipliers weight that effect.

</details>

---

### Misconception 11: "Newton is never used at scale."

<details>
<summary><b>Click for Reality Check</b></summary>

| Misconception | Reality |
|---------------|---------|
| Newton is impossible for deep learning. | The explicit Hessian is never formed, but **Hessian-vector products** cost ≈ one extra backward pass, and structured approximations (K-FAC, Shampoo) are deployed at scale. The accurate claim: "we never form the explicit Hessian; we sometimes use structured approximations." |

**The stronger claim is false.** Second-order information **is** used at scale, just not the full Hessian.

</details>

---

### Misconception 12: "ℓ₁ is sparse because it's smaller."

<details>
<summary><b>Click for Reality Check</b></summary>

| Misconception | Reality |
|---------------|---------|
| ℓ₁ penalty is smaller, so it pushes weights to zero. | ℓ₁ is sparse because the **ball has vertices on the axes** (geometric), or because `∂|·|(0) = [-1,1]` is an interval (algebraic). A smooth penalty has a single slope at 0 and can only shrink, never zero out. |

**The key:** The subdifferential at the kink is **set-valued**, making `0 ∈ ∂f(x*)` easier to satisfy than `∇f(x*) = 0`. This is the exact mechanism behind sparsity.

</details>

---

### Misconception 13: "Loss flattening means convergence."

<details>
<summary><b>Click for Reality Check</b></summary>

| Misconception | Reality |
|---------------|---------|
| Flat loss = we found a minimum. | Could be a **noise floor** (constant-step SGD bouncing), a **plateau** (structural issue, not converging), a **saddle** (temporary, will resume), or a **capacity limit**. Diagnosis: drop LR by 10×. If loss falls further, it was the noise floor. |

</details>

---

### Misconception 14: "Optimization is minimizing f directly."

<details>
<summary><b>Click for Reality Check</b></summary>

| Misconception | Reality |
|---------------|---------|
| We minimize f. | We minimize a **truncated Taylor model** of f. Order 1 → direction (needs LR). Order 2 → complete step (Newton). The remainder must be checked. |

**The sentence to carry:** "You never optimize f — you optimize a truncated Taylor model of it."

</details>

---

### Misconception 15: "The learning rate is just a tuning knob."

<details>
<summary><b>Click for Reality Check</b></summary>

| Misconception | Reality |
|---------------|---------|
| LR is a hyperparameter you guess. | The LR is **how far you trust a bound**. The descent lemma guarantees decrease for `t < 2/L`. The best t sits near the cliff (t ≈ t_div/2 to t_div/10). It's not arbitrary — it's respecting a curvature bound. |

**The learning-rate finder:** Find the cliff, back off. The cliff is the bound, made visible.

</details>

## 6. REVISION QUESTIONS — OPTIMIZATION

### Section 1: Foundations

<details>
<summary><b>O-1: What are the 9 core ideas of optimization?</b></summary>

1. Optimality is local — only convexity upgrades local to global
2. You optimize Taylor models, not f
3. LR exists because a linear model has no bottom
4. Curvature caps the step (`t < 2/L`)
5. κ is the tax (`κ = λ_max/λ_min`)
6. "Steepest" is a choice (metric)
7. Noise buys cheapness, costs accuracy
8. Constraints weaken the condition on ∇f
9. A multiplier is a price

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>O-2: What are the three characterizations of convexity?</b></summary>

1. Chord above graph (no derivatives needed)
2. Tangent below graph (requires differentiability)
3. Hessian ⪰ 0 (requires twice differentiability)

**Why it matters:** A convex function with ∇f = 0 has a **global minimum**. Local information becomes global.

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>O-3: Why is ∇f perpendicular to its level set?</b></summary>

Walking along a level set, f is constant, so the directional derivative is zero. Hence ∇f has no component along the level set — it's perpendicular to it.

**This is the entire geometric content of Lagrange multipliers.**

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>O-4: What does `vᵀHv` mean?</b></summary>

`vᵀHv` is the curvature you feel walking along direction v. It's the second derivative of f along the line through x in direction v.

`Hv` is how the gradient changes when you step along v.

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>O-5: What is the condition number κ and why does it matter?</b></summary>

`κ = λ_max/λ_min`. The stiffest direction caps the step; the flattest sets progress. The mismatch costs a factor κ.

- GD: `2.3κ` steps per decade
- Momentum: `2.3√κ` steps per decade
- Newton: blind to κ

**Everything clever either reduces κ or becomes blind to it.**

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>O-6: What are the 4 landscape types and their spectra?</b></summary>

| Shape | Spectrum | What it is |
|-------|----------|------------|
| Bowl | all λ > 0, small κ | Fast convergence |
| Valley | all λ > 0, huge κ | Slow, zigzag |
| Saddle | mixed signs | Temporary stall |
| Plateau | λ ≈ 0 over a region | Structural issue |

</details>

---

### Section 2: First-Order Methods

<details>
<summary><b>O-7: What is the descent lemma and why is it the most used inequality?</b></summary>

`f(x+d) ≤ f(x) + ∇f(x)ᵀd + (L/2)||d||²`

- The function is trapped under a parabola
- Minimizing the bound gives `d = -(1/L)∇f`
- Safe range: `t < 2/L`
- Guaranteed decrease: `f(x_k) - f(x_{k+1}) ≥ (1/(2L))||∇f||²`

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>O-8: What are the GD rates in each setting?</b></summary>

| Setting | Rate | Iterations |
|---------|------|------------|
| Convex, L-smooth | `O(1/k)` | `O(1/ε)` |
| Strongly convex | linear | `O(κ log(1/ε))` |
| Non-convex | `min ||∇f||²` | `O(ε⁻²)` |
| SGD (non-convex) | same + noise | `O(ε⁻⁴)` |

**The non-convex row is the one that applies to deep learning.**

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>O-9: What is momentum and how does it work?</b></summary>

**Mechanism:** Unroll `w_k = Σ βʲ g_{k-j}` — steady gradients amplify by `1/(1-β)` (≈10 at β=0.9), alternating gradients damp by `1/(1+β)` (≈0.53).

**Rate:** Replaces κ by √κ — `O(√κ log(1/ε))`.

**The ratio is 19 at β=0.9** — momentum favours agreement across steps.

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>O-10: What are the Wolfe conditions and why do they matter?</b></summary>

**Armijo (A):** `f(x+td) ≤ f(x) + c₁·t·∇fᵀd` — forbids steps too long

**Curvature (C):** `∇f(x+td)ᵀd ≥ c₂·∇f(x)ᵀd` — forbids steps too short

**Why they matter for BFGS:** Wolfe (C) implies `yᵀs > 0`, which implies `H_{k+1} ≻ 0`, which implies descent. The chain makes BFGS converge.

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>O-11: What is the one template that covers all optimizers?</b></summary>

`x_{k+1} = x_k - t_k B_k^{-1} ∇f(x_k)`

| B_k | Method |
|-----|--------|
| I | Gradient Descent |
| I (with EMA gradient) | Momentum |
| diag(√Σg²) | AdaGrad |
| diag(√EMA(g²)) | RMSProp |
| diag(√EMA(g²)) + EMA(g) | Adam |
| Adam + decay outside | AdamW |
| Hessian | Newton |
| Estimated Hessian | BFGS / L-BFGS |

**Every method is one algorithm + one metric choice.**

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>O-12: What is the adaptive family chain?</b></summary>

**SGD** → one step size for all coordinates

**AdaGrad** → per-coordinate scaling, but sum only grows → step decays to zero

**RMSProp** → replace sum with average (forgets)

**Adam** → add momentum + bias correction

**AMSGrad** → fix Adam's non-convergence

**AdamW** → decouple weight decay

Each fixes a named defect of its predecessor.

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>O-13: What does Adam actually do?</b></summary>

- `m_k` estimates `E[g]` (direction)
- `v_k` estimates `E[g²]` (scale)
- Ratio `m̂/√v̂` is a **signal-to-noise ratio**
- Consistent gradients → full steps
- Noise-dominated → damped

**Adam is NOT second-order:** it divides by the size of the gradient, not by curvature.

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>O-14: Why does Adam need bias correction?</b></summary>

`m_0 = v_0 = 0` (placeholders, not measurements). Unrolling:

`v_k = (1-β₂)Σ β₂ʲ g²_{k-j}`

Taking expectations: `E[v_k] = (1-β₂^k)E[g²]`

So `v_k` is too small by `1-β₂^k` — divide by it to make it unbiased.

**At k=1, uncorrected v₁ is 1000× too small** (β₂=0.999).

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>O-15: Why does AdamW exist?</b></summary>

With Adam, an L2 penalty passes through the per-coordinate rescaling:

`x ← x - t·(g + λx)/√v`

Coordinates with large gradients get **less decay** — not what "weight decay" means.

**AdamW:** `x ← x - t·m̂/(√v̂+ε) - t·λ·x` — decay outside the rescaling.

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>O-16: What is the variance of a minibatch gradient?</b></summary>

`Var(ĝ_B) = σ²/B` — quadrupling the batch halves the noise.

**Unbiasedness:** `E[ĝ_B] = ∇F` at fixed θ.

**Noise floor:** With constant step, iterates random-walk in a ball of size `O(tσ²/(mB))`. Halving LR shrinks the ball by √2, not 2.

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>O-17: Why does noise square the cost?</b></summary>

Deterministic: `O(ε⁻²)` — take η as large as L permits

Stochastic: `O(ε⁻⁴)` — two competing terms:

`1/K` (progress) vs `σ²` (noise)

Balancing them gives `η* ∝ 1/√K` and `O(1/√K)` bound, hence `O(ε⁻⁴)`.

**This exponent is unimprovable** for bounded-variance stochastic first-order methods.

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>O-18: What are the Robbins–Monro conditions?</b></summary>

1. `Σ t_k = ∞` — you must travel far enough
2. `Σ t_k² < ∞` — accumulated noise must be finite

**The step must decay, but not too fast.**

Inverse-√k: `Σ t_k = ∞` ✓, `Σ t_k² = ∞` ✗ — sits just outside the range.

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>O-19: What are the two reasons for warmup?</b></summary>

**Reason 1 (adaptive only):** `v_k` is a one-sample estimate at step 1, so `m̂/√v̂` is noise. β₂=0.999 means memory of ≈1000 steps — first ~1000 steps are fragile.

**Reason 2 (any method):** Sharpness L is largest at initialization, so safe step `2/L` is smallest at the start. Warmup respects a bound you can't compute.

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>O-20: What is gradient clipping and why is it not a hack?</b></summary>

Norm clipping: `g ← g · min(1, c/||g||)` — preserves direction, caps magnitude.

**Why it's not a hack:** Under `(L₀,L₁)`-smoothness (curvature grows with gradient norm), clipping is the **provably better algorithm** than fixed-step GD.

The classical theory's assumption (global L) was the wrong model.

</details>

---

### Section 3: Second-Order Methods

<details>
<summary><b>O-21: Derive Newton's method.</b></summary>

Minimize the order-2 Taylor model:

`m_k(d) = f(x_k) + ∇f(x_k)ᵀd + (1/2)dᵀH_kd`

Set derivative to zero: `H_k d = -∇f(x_k)`

So `d = -H_k⁻¹∇f(x_k)` — a complete step with no learning rate.

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>O-22: What is affine invariance and why does Newton have it?</b></summary>

Change variables `x = Ay`, `g(y) = f(Ay)`.

Newton step in y-space: `dy = -A⁻¹H⁻¹A^{-⊤}·A⊤∇f = A⁻¹(-H⁻¹∇f) = A⁻¹dx`.

**Newton visits exactly the same points** regardless of scaling — blind to κ.

Gradient descent does not have this property.

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>O-23: What is the secant equation and why does it matter?</b></summary>

`B_{k+1}s_k = y_k` where `s_k = x_{k+1}-x_k`, `y_k = ∇f(x_{k+1})-∇f(x_k)`.

**Read it as:** The curvature model must reproduce the gradient change you just observed.

**Why Wolfe matters:** Wolfe (C) implies `yᵀs > 0`; this implies `H_{k+1} ≻ 0`; this implies descent.

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>O-24: What is a trust region and how does ν interpolate Newton and GD?</b></summary>

Minimize `m_k(d)` subject to `||d|| ≤ Δ_k`.

The solution is characterized by `(B + νI)d = -∇f`, `ν ≥ 0`, `ν(||d|| - Δ) = 0`.

- `ν = 0` → Newton (constraint inactive)
- `ν → ∞` → gradient descent (tiny step)

**One equation interpolates the entire chapter.**

</details>

---

### Section 4: Constrained Optimization

<details>
<summary><b>O-25: What is the one sentence of constrained optimization?</b></summary>

**A point is optimal exactly when there is no feasible direction that decreases the objective.**

- Unconstrained: ∇f = 0
- Equalities: ∇f in span of ∇h_j (Lagrange)
- Inequalities: -∇f in cone of ∇g_i (KKT)

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>O-26: Derive Lagrange multipliers in 4 steps.</b></summary>

1. ∇h is normal to its level set (h is constant along it)
2. Optimality makes ∇f normal to the surface (no component along it)
3. Both lie in the same normal space
4. `null(Jh)⊥ = range(Jh⊤)` → ∇f = Σ λ_j ∇h_j

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>O-27: What are the 4 KKT conditions?</b></summary>

1. **Stationarity:** `∇f + Σ µ_i∇g_i + Σ λ_j∇h_j = 0`
2. **Primal feasibility:** `g_i(x) ≤ 0`, `h_j(x) = 0`
3. **Dual feasibility:** `µ_i ≥ 0` (walls push, never pull)
4. **Complementary slackness:** `µ_i·g_i(x) = 0`

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>O-28: What is complementary slackness and why does it matter?</b></summary>

`µ_i·g_i(x) = 0` — only touched walls exert force.

- If `g_i < 0` (inactive) → `µ_i = 0` (no force)
- If `µ_i > 0` → `g_i = 0` (wall is touched)

**In SVM:** Points beyond margin have `g_i < 0` → `α_i = 0` → they drop out of `w = Σ α_i y_i x_i`. Sparsity is **derived**, not designed.

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>O-29: What is the difference between necessity and sufficiency in KKT?</b></summary>

**Necessity** (minimum → KKT) needs a **constraint qualification** (LICQ, MFCQ, Slater).

**Sufficiency** (KKT → minimum) needs **convexity**.

**Conflating them is the most common error in constrained optimization.**

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>O-30: What is a shadow price?</b></summary>

`µ*_i = -∂p*/∂u_i` — how much the optimal value improves per unit you relax constraint i.

- Large µ = expensive, tightly binding constraint — go negotiate this one
- Zero µ = not binding — loosening it buys nothing

**Complementary slackness, read economically.**

</details>

---

### Section 5: ML Payoff

<details>
<summary><b>O-31: Why is SVM sparsity not designed but derived?</b></summary>

Points beyond the margin have `g_i < 0` (inactive constraint), so by complementary slackness `α_i = 0`. They drop out of `w = Σ α_i y_i x_i`.

The model depends only on support vectors — points on the margin.

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>O-32: What are the 3 readings of weight decay?</b></summary>

The same λ is:

1. A **Lagrange multiplier** of `||w||² ≤ C`
2. A **shadow price** of that norm budget
3. An **inverse prior variance** (Gaussian prior, MAP)

**One scalar, three readings.** Exact for convex losses, analogy for deep nets.

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>O-33: Why is backpropagation the adjoint method?</b></summary>

Write training as constrained problem: minimize L(z_N) subject to `z_k = f_k(z_{k-1}, θ_k)`.

The backward recursion `λ_k = J_{k+1}⊤λ_{k+1}` is solving the **adjoint system**.

**The backpropagated deltas are Lagrange multipliers** of the layer constraints.

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>O-34: Why is softmax constrained entropy maximization?</b></summary>

Maximize `-Σ p_k log p_k` subject to `Σ p_k = 1` and `Σ p_k z_k = constant`.

Stationarity: `-log p_k - 1 + λ_1 + λ_2 z_k = 0` → `p_k ∝ e^{λ_2 z_k}`.

The normalizing denominator is the exponentiated multiplier of the sum-to-one constraint.

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>O-35: What is the implicit bias of gradient descent?</b></summary>

On linearly separable data, unregularized logistic regression with GD converges in **direction** to the hard-margin SVM solution.

The algorithm supplies a margin the objective never asked for. The norm grows like log k, so the direction converges slowly.

**Proved for linear predictors, analogy for deep nets.**

</details>

---

### Section 6: Diagnostics

<details>
<summary><b>O-36: What are the 5 loss curve signatures?</b></summary>

1. **Healthy:** straight on log axis → let it run
2. **Crawling:** almost flat → LR too small → multiply by 3
3. **Plateau:** fast drop, hard floor → dead units or conditioning → check activations
4. **Spiking:** vertical jumps → rare large gradients → clip
5. **Diverging:** monotone increase → LR too high → halve until stable

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>O-37: What is the most valuable debugging test?</b></summary>

**Overfit eight examples to zero loss.** A correct model-plus-optimizer does this in a few hundred steps.

- If it **can't** → you have a bug (loss, labels, masking, wiring)
- If it **can** → pipeline is sound — every remaining problem is optimization, regularization, or data

**Run this before any tuning.**

</details>

---

<details>
<summary><b>O-38: When should you stop tuning the learning rate?</b></summary>

When train loss falls and validation loss rises, optimization is no longer your bottleneck.

No optimizer choice fixes anything to the right of that line — that's a regularization, data, or capacity decision.

</details>

---

### Section 7: The Complete Cheat Sheet

<details>
<summary><b>O-39: Complete Cheat Sheet</b></summary>

**The 5 Sentences:**
1. Optimization is moving downhill efficiently; with constraints, downhill only inside feasible set
2. You optimize Taylor models, not f; every method is `x_{k+1} = x_k - t_kB_k^{-1}∇f(x_k)`
3. κ = λ_max/λ_min — everything clever reduces it or becomes blind to it
4. Constrained optimality: no feasible descent direction; multiplier = price
5. In DL: mechanisms survive, guarantees don't — know which you're quoting

**The 3 Numbers:**
- `t < 2/L` is the safe range
- Momentum effective step: `t/(1-β)`
- Minibatch variance: `σ²/B`

**The 4 KKT Conditions:**
1. Stationarity
2. Primal feasibility
3. Dual feasibility (µ ≥ 0)
4. Complementary slackness (µ·g = 0)

**The 5 Loss Curves:**
Healthy, Crawling, Plateau, Spiking, Diverging

</details>
